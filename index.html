No. 01 The promise

Everything you enter stays on your device.

There's no account to make and no server to sync with, so nothing you log ever leaves your phone. I don't collect anything, which means there's nothing to sell either.

No. 02 The record

Track what actually happened, not what you can remember.

Chronic pain is hard to describe from memory and appointments are short. Rootwork keeps the record so you can point at something real.

Find out what is actually moving your pain.

Check in once a day with your sleep, energy, mood and stress, and Rootwork compares each one against your pain. It's all based on your own history, not averages from a study.

Measured in pain points, not statistics

Results are shown as points on your own 0–10 scale, like "1.8 points higher after a short night", instead of statistics you'd need to look up.

Lagged where lag makes sense

Sleep, stress and activity are compared against the next day's pain. Comparing last night's sleep with last night's pain mostly just shows that pain ruins sleep.

It tells you when it doesn't know

It won't show anything until it has 14 days to go on, and if something isn't affecting your pain it'll say so. I'd rather it gave no answer than a wrong one.

The same table, as a spreadsheet

You can export it all as a spreadsheet, one row per day. It's the same data the app uses to work these out.

No. 04 The Sanctuary

Somewhere to go that isn't about being ill.

A small 3D farm inside the app. Clear a plot, plant things, keep bees, and watch the weather and light change through the day. Logging earns you a few coins to spend there.

Nothing here punishes you

Nothing times out, nothing dies because you were too sick to open the app, and there's no streak to lose. Come back after a month and it's exactly how you left it. That was the main rule going in.

It knows what time it is

It follows real daylight, with lanterns at dusk and fireflies at night. The weather comes from the date, so everyone gets the same rain on the same day without needing a server.

No. 05 Gardens

An app you can stand to look at on a bad day.

Twelve colour themes, each with its own little animations, like fireflies at midnight or petals in spring. Tap one and this page switches to it. There are a few more in the app that you unlock or stumble on, but I'm not going to spoil them here.

No. 06 PulsePoints

Medicine that's going somewhere.

A small blog inside the app with good news from medicine, for when everything else you read about being sick is bad. The posts are here too, so you can send one to someone.

It's off by default in the app. If you turn it on, it downloads one public file a day, with no account, cookies or tracking.

No. 09 Questions

A few things people ask.

Is it really free, or free for now?
Yes. There's no paid version planned, no payment code in the app, and no accounts to attach one to.
If there's no server, how do I move to a new phone?
Export a file on the old phone and import it on the new one. It's manual on purpose, because syncing would mean keeping a copy of your health data on a server somewhere.
What happens if I lose my phone?
Then the data's gone unless you exported it. That's the downside of not having a server, so export every so often and keep the file somewhere safe.
Does it ever connect to the internet?
Only if you turn something on. The barometric pressure feature and PulsePoints both download public data when enabled, and both are off by default. Nothing about you gets sent.
Can it tell me what's causing my pain?
No. It shows what tends to happen together in your own log, like "your pain is two points lower after a long night", but that doesn't prove one causes the other.
Will my doctor actually look at this?
Probably more than they'd look at your phone. There's a one-page printout made for short appointments, and a CSV if they want the numbers.
No. 10 Small print

Rootwork is a personal record-keeping tool, not a medical device. It doesn't diagnose, treat or give medical advice. It only shows what you logged, which isn't the same as a clinical finding. Talk to a doctor about your symptoms and treatment.
